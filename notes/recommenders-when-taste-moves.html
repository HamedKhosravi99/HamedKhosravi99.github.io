Recommender systems when taste moves

A recommender gets one number back per impression: clicked, or did not. From that it has to work out the shape of what someone likes — while the shape is changing.

A catalogue has far too many dimensions to learn one at a time. The reason recommenders work at all is that taste is not spread across all of them: it is driven by a handful of latent factors, and everything a shopper responds to lives in the low-dimensional space those factors span. Find that space and you are estimating four or five numbers instead of sixty. Miss it and you are estimating sixty.

Two things make finding it hard. The space moves — it drifts, and occasionally turns sharply, at moments nobody announces. And you never observe it directly. You put one item in front of one shopper and get back a single scalar, which is the projection of their whole taste onto that one item, plus noise.

So the recommender runs a loop: probe to generate information it cannot get from serving its best guess, identify the space from the scalars those probes return, then exploit by projecting the catalogue into it and ranking there. Watch it.

The filled blue plane is the space the shopper’s taste actually lives in, with the dark red arrow wandering inside it — that is their preference moving around within a structure that is, for now, holding still. The green dashed outline is the plane the recommender has identified and is ranking the catalogue in. Every tall red tick on the timeline is a probe: an impression deliberately spent on something other than the best guess, because serving the best guess teaches you almost nothing about the rest of the space.

Each probe returns one scalar. On its own that is almost no information — but squared, it is a rank-one measurement of the plane, and enough of them stacked up identify it. The angle closes like 1/√m in the number of probes, which is why the green outline swings onto the blue quickly at first and then crawls the last few degrees.

The two sliders are the two things you actually control, and they do different jobs. Spending a larger share of impressions on probes fills the timeline with red and gets you there sooner — it buys speed. Noise does something you cannot buy your way out of: it sets a floor on how close the estimate can ever get, shown in the readout above. Push the noise to 0.80 and the floor goes past 20°; no probe budget closes that gap, because the information is not in the data to begin with.

Then a blue dashed line crosses the timeline. The plane has turned, and everything the recommender knows is now evidence about a world that is gone — you can watch the green outline sitting at the old angle while the blue plane has moved out from under it. Its errors grow; the detector climbs; and when it crosses b the estimate is thrown away rather than updated. That is the green dot. Rebuilding costs probes all over again, which is why you cannot simply reset constantly, and why the detector has to fire on real turns rather than on noise.

Try your own catalogue

The trade is always the same shape: probes cost real impressions now, and buy narrower intervals later. Whether that is worth doing depends on your catalogue, your noise, and — mostly — how often your users change their minds.

The number that decides it is the last row. If your taste structure turns more often than the probing takes to pay for itself, you never get to the part where you profit; you just pay the entry fee repeatedly. That is the regime where a recommender should probably stop pretending it can track the structure and fall back on something cheaper.
