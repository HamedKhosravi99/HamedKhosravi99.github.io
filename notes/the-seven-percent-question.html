The 7% question

Threadline gives up 7% of a nine-day campaign to find out what its shoppers want. Whether that traffic buys anything is settled before the campaign starts — and not by anyone who thinks of themselves as doing statistics.

Threadline is a fashion retailer running its Autumn Edit. The recommender scores items on twenty product attributes — price position, brand group, style group, merchandising family — and the campaign runs for nine days. Traffic arrives in slices of ten thousand impressions, which matters: a single impression is a click or no click, and you cannot estimate a variance from a coin flip. Batch it and the click rate of a slice is approximately Gaussian around the baseline of 34.7 bp, with a known noise level of 5.88 bp. That is not a modelling convenience; it is how the decision is actually taken. You commit a configuration to a slice of traffic, never to one shopper.

Seven percent of those slices are given to probes: deliberately randomised slates that serve something other than the current best guess. That is real money. The question the campaign exists to answer is whether it comes back.

Each line below is one attribute. Its length is how precisely nine days of traffic has pinned down that attribute’s effect on click-through — short is confident, and the dashed line at the top is what knowing nothing looks like. Watch the campaign run.

With nothing contracted, every line shortens as evidence lands. Then day five arrives and they all jump back out. The latent factors behind shopper preference moved — a competitor’s drop, a cold snap, who knows — and nothing announced it. The campaign has no event to react to. It simply discovers that what it had learned is now about a world that no longer exists, and starts again with four days left.

What the plan already decided

Now push the slider. Those attributes stop moving.

A merchandising plan fixes the price mix and the contracted placements before a campaign opens. Entry-price inventory is a set share of the slate. The promo slot is sold. Those are commercial commitments, made months earlier, by people who were not asked a question about identifiability.

But a probe can only teach you about a direction it is able to move along. If the plan holds the price mix fixed, then no slate the campaign is allowed to serve varies the price mix — and the effect of price position is not estimated badly, it is not estimated at all. Its interval sits exactly where it started on day one and stays there through all nine days and every probe. More traffic does nothing. A longer campaign does nothing.

This is the difference between a noisy estimate and no estimate, and it is invisible in the usual reporting. The campaign still returns a number for price position. The number is the prior.

The part that is genuinely counter-intuitive

Watch the free attributes as you contract more. They get sharper.

Of course they do: the same probe budget is spread over fewer directions, so each one gets more of it. Contracting half the catalogue makes your measurements of the other half look excellent. A dashboard reporting average interval width across what it measured would show the plan improving as it removes things from the campaign’s reach.

That is the trap the case is built around. The exploration budget is not wasted in a way that shows up as noise. It is wasted in a way that shows up as confidence.

Where the method comes in

Two things make this tractable rather than hopeless. Preferences are not twenty independent dials: they are driven by a handful of latent factors, so if the structure really is low-rank you relearn three numbers after a shift rather than twenty, and every interval narrows by √(d/r). That is the whole prize, and the widget above already assumes you have it.

Earning it is the hard part, because you never see the reward of the item you did not show. Catching a Moving Subspace works out when a rotating low-rank subspace can be recovered from single-play rewards at all: it needs a known noise variance, bounded rewards, and probe support that spans the space. The first two Threadline gets for free by batching traffic into slices. The third is the one the merchandising plan takes away.
