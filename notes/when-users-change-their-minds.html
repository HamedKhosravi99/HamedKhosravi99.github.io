When your users change their minds

A recommender works out the direction your taste points in. Then your taste turns, and nobody sends it a memo.

Recommenders are, underneath, estimators of a direction. Watch a few hundred interactions, work out roughly what a user is pointed at, then serve them things pointed the same way. It works, and when it works it keeps working — right up until the user takes a new job, has a kid, or gets into cycling.

Nothing announces that. There is no event, no flag, no retraining trigger. There is only a stream of clicks that used to be predictable and is now a little worse than it was. And a model that fits on everything it has ever seen is, by construction, still defending the old direction — the longer ago the change, the more stale evidence it is averaging in.

Set the slider to never first. All three finish within a few points of each other. This matters: the detector is not buying its advantage by being cleverer in general, and it is not free money. When there is nothing to detect, detecting costs essentially nothing — which is the property you actually want before you put a reset rule anywhere near production.

Now start moving it. Everything separates, and the interesting failure is the middle one.

Averaging everything is not the safe option

“Never update” fails in the way you expect: it is wrong from the first turn and stays wrong. Nobody ships that. What people do ship is the cautious-looking one — refit on all the data, all the time, because throwing data away feels reckless.

But look at the gap between the dashed line and the teal one. Refitting on everything does adapt; it just adapts slowly, and it is dragged by exactly the history that is no longer true. Every stale interaction is a vote for the direction the user has already left. Being cautious with data is the thing that makes it slow to recover, and it never fully recovers before the next turn arrives.

Throwing history away is the aggressive-looking move and the correct one — provided you only do it when something actually changed. That proviso is the whole problem, because the change is not observed. All the model sees is its own errors getting worse, which is also what noise looks like.

What the paper does

The widget uses a deliberately crude detector — compare recent prediction errors against slightly older ones, reset when the ratio jumps — because it runs in your browser while you drag a slider. The real problem is harder in two ways the toy version skips.

First, preferences do not live in two dimensions. They live in a high-dimensional space with a low-rank structure, and it is that structure, not a single direction, that moves. Low rank is what makes the problem learnable at all — it keeps the number of things you have to estimate small enough to estimate quickly, which is exactly what you need when you keep having to start over.

Second, you never observe the reward of the item you did not show. You get one scalar per round, for the one thing you served. Catching a Moving Subspace works out how to identify a rotating low-rank subspace from those scalars alone, using priced probes to keep the estimate identifiable, and a two-window spectral detector — the principled version of the one above — to decide when the subspace has moved and the estimate should be reset rather than extended.
